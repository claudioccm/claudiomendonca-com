MIT put a number on something most teams already suspected: the large majority of corporate AI pilots never produce a measurable return. The figure gets quoted as a verdict on the technology. It isn't. It's a verdict on how the technology gets deployed.

## It was never the model

The pilots that fail rarely fail because the model is bad. They fail because nobody decided what "done" looks like, who owns the output, or which existing process the AI is supposed to replace. A model dropped next to a broken workflow inherits the broken workflow.

The pilots that work share an unglamorous trait: they start from a specific, repeating task with a clear owner and a clear definition of success. Not "use AI in marketing." Something like "draft the Monday client digest from these four sources, in our voice, for Sam to approve by 9am."

## The boring part is the product

Scope it that tightly and the model almost stops mattering. What matters is the plumbing around it — the sources, the format, the approval step, the schedule. That's the 5% of projects that return something. They didn't buy a smarter model. They bought a smaller, sharper problem.
