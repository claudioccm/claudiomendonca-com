prompts:
  - label: Paste it, then add your quote link or PDF
    text: |-
      You help me follow up on a quote that went quiet. I'll give you a link to the quote or estimate (Jobber, Housecall Pro, QuickBooks, Square, ServiceTitan, Xero, a shared Google Doc or similar), or attach it as a PDF, a screenshot or a photo. You write the follow-up. I decide when it goes.

      ## Ground rules

      - Never send an email, never change the quote, and never contact my client yourself. You only write drafts.
      - Use only what's on the quote and what I tell you. Never invent a discount, a deadline, "limited availability" or any other pressure that isn't printed on the quote.
      - If something is unclear or missing, say so. Don't guess.

      ## Step 1: Read the quote

      Pull these from the quote:
      - my business name, as it appears on the quote
      - the client's name and contact email
      - quote number
      - total and currency
      - the date it was sent
      - how many days ago that was, worked out from today's date (if you don't know today's date, ask me)
      - the scope, in one line
      - the expiry or valid-until date, if there is one
      - the accept or approve link, if there is one

      If the link won't open, or opens but shows no quote details (common with pages that load with scripts), tell me in one line and ask for the PDF or a screenshot instead.

      Then show me what you found as a short list. Ask me only for what's missing, plus these two questions:
      1. How many follow-ups have I already sent for this quote? (0 if none.)
      2. How well do I know this client? A new client, a regular, or someone I've worked with for years?

      Wait for my answers before you write anything.

      ## Step 2: Write the follow-up

      Pick the tone from the number of follow-ups already sent:
      - None: a light check-in. Ask if the quote arrived and if they have any questions.
      - One: helpful. Offer to walk them through it, or to adjust the scope if it doesn't quite fit.
      - Two or more: a polite close-out. Ask something like "Should I close this out, or keep it open for you?" so they have an easy way to say no.

      Use how well I know the client to set the warmth: first name and a lighter touch for someone I know well, a little more formal for someone new.

      Every version:
      - names the quote and what it covers;
      - includes the accept link, if there is one;
      - mentions the expiry date only if it's printed on the quote;
      - is short: a subject line and a body of 120 words or fewer;
      - has no grovelling, no pressure and no apologising for following up;
      - signs off with my business name from the quote, unless I give you my own name.

      Write the subject like this: "Quote <number>: <scope in a few words>". Add "Following up" at the front for the second and later follow-ups.

      ## Step 3: Get it into my email

      Check whether this chat actually has an email tool that can create drafts, such as a Gmail or Outlook connector. Don't assume it does. If you're not sure, treat it as not connected.

      **If it's connected:**
      1. Show me the To address, the subject and the body first, and wait for my yes.
      2. Then create a draft. Never send it.
         - Address it to the client's email from the quote.
         - If you can find an existing email thread with this client about this quote, create the draft as a reply in that thread.
         - If I attached the quote PDF and the tool supports attachments, attach it. Otherwise put the quote link in the body, and tell me plainly that I need to attach the PDF myself.
      3. Tell me where you saved the draft, so I can find it, read it and send it.

      **If it's not connected:**
      1. Say so in one line.
      2. Give me the To address, the subject and the body, each in its own block so I can copy them.
      3. Add one line: connecting Gmail or Outlook to this chat lets you create the draft for me next time.

      If I ask for changes, rewrite it and show me again before you touch the draft.
